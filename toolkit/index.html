Who's holding what now? Pad toolkit

From the talk Who's holding what now? New tools, shifting burdens and learning to keep up, MoT London, Nov 2026.

Nineteen small experiments for putting the talk into practice, in your team, your organisation or the community. Pick one, try it, adapt it, put it down. Then tell someone what happened.

If you missed the talk

  • Apples: what a new tool gives you. Time, output, speed.

  • Pad: the cushion on Atlas's shoulders in the oldest carving of the myth. Whatever makes a load holdable: time, practice, help.

  • The garden: the context only experience gives you. Why something matters, where it breaks, who to ask.

  • Iolaus: Heracles' nephew, who helped him beat the Hydra. The king said the labour didn't count because he'd had help.

How to use this toolkit

Almost none of this is new. Most of these tools are other people's ideas, borrowed and adapted through my own lens. Where a tool has a well-known cousin, it's listed under Roots, so you can look it up and learn from the people who got there first.

Run each one as an experiment, not an argument. Don't argue for a change; propose one of these as a time-boxed experiment with a kill rule. Run it like you'd test anything:

  1. Hypothesis: what do you expect it to change?

  2. Smallest version: one person, one sprint, one meeting.

  3. Measure: each tool suggests something to watch. Pick one, and decide what you'd expect to see.

  4. Time box and kill rule: when do you stop, and what would make you stop early?

  5. Write it down and share it (see the lab notebook, below). A failed experiment that's shared is a pad for someone else.

You get what you measure for. The measures here are signals to talk about, not targets. Keep them private or team-level. The moment one becomes a target, people start working to the number instead of the point.

House rules:

  • Choose, don't impose. A pad you're made to wear isn't a pad. Offer tools; let people pick.

  • Not out of hours. If it only works in people's evenings, it's more apples, not a pad.

  • Not finding success is data, not failure.

  • One pad, many shoulders. Dropped kerbs were built for wheelchairs; now everyone with a pram, a suitcase or a bike uses them. Every tool here works at more than one level.

How far it goes: each tool shows the same idea at different levels of ownership.

  • You: start on Monday, no permission needed.

  • Your team: agree it in the room.

  • Your org: needs backing, budget or a policy.

  • Out there: meetups, MoT, the wider community.

The lab notebook

The one tool that holds the others together.

  • You: try a tool (any tool in this kit, or a new AI tool) for a week. Five minutes back to the team: what did it do well, what did it hand back, would you keep it? Ask again a month later.

  • Your team: one shared page. Hypothesis, what happened, would we do it again. Skim it together monthly.

  • Out there: pick a few tools at a meetup, run them in your teams, and report back on a named date with a named host.

Watch: nobody reads it; novelty bias; experiments that never end.

Measure: how many experiments reach a decision (keep, change or stop), rather than drifting on.

Roots: safe-to-fail probes (Dave Snowden, Cynefin); Toyota Kata; spikes; the Thoughtworks Tech Radar; MoT's 30 Days of Testing; Elisabeth Hocke's testing tour.

Decide on purpose

The freed time and the changed ways of working are both choices. If nobody makes them, the deadline does.

1. The apple cycle

What it's for: deciding on purpose before a tool lands, checking what actually happened, and letting go of habits the old tools forced on us.

How:

  1. Before (pre-mortem): "It's six months from now and this made things worse. Why?" Then: "If it works, what do we do with the time? How will roles change?"

  2. Agree the split: some to delivery, some to pads (learning, pairing, strategic work, upskilling, rest). Write it down.

  3. During (retro prompt): "What did the tool hand back, and who's holding it?"

  4. After (30, 60, 90 days): compare the plan, the retro notes and reality. Include people who weren't in the decision.

  5. Each quarter (unlearning): "Which of our habits are now obsolete?" Let a junior chair it, and both sides surface their assumptions. Ask why the fence is there before pulling it down.

How far it goes:

  • You: ask the pre-mortem question in the next tool conversation you're in.

  • Your team: agree the split before the tool lands, and check it at retro.

  • Your org: say what the time is for. Managers are absorbing these changes; give them a plan to hold.

Watch: the split gets agreed and then ignored; pre-mortems used to kill tools rather than shape them; only happy answers reported upwards; cutting things nobody remembers the reason for.

Measure: at 90 days, how much of the freed time went where you agreed, and how much the deadline took.

Why: faster delivery isn't always the best return.

  • Ranganathan & Ye, AI Doesn't Reduce Work—It Intensifies It (HBR, 2026): without an "AI practice" of pauses and sequencing, people simply took on more. HBR

  • Gallup 2026: engagement was 43% where employers had clearly explained their AI plan, and 28% where they hadn't. Gallup

  • Gary Klein, Performing a Project Premortem (HBR, 2007). HBR

Roots: the pre-mortem (Klein); retrospectives; after-action reviews (US Army); post-implementation reviews; Chesterton's fence; start, stop, continue.

2. Protected pads

What it's for: ring-fencing some of the apples so they don't all turn into more work.

How: name some freed capacity as protected, and say what it's for:

  • Learning: a fixed slot each week or fortnight. Any tool in this kit can live here.

  • Glue work: explicit time in the plan for mentoring, pairing and teaching. Rotate it so it doesn't land on the same people.

  • Rest: don't reward good work with more work. Recovery, thinking time, the backlog of annoyances. More than a pizza party.

How far it goes:

  • You: block one hour a fortnight and say out loud what it's for.

  • Your team: a standing pad afternoon, and glue work as visible tickets in the plan.

  • Your org (the apple tax): any business case for a tool ring-fences a fixed percentage of the projected saving for training and mentoring.

Watch: it's the first thing cancelled (that's the signal it's needed); it's hard to justify upwards (frame it as sustainability and fewer mistakes); glue work falling on the same few people; the projected saving was invented in the first place, which is also worth finding out.

Measure: how often the protected time actually happens, versus being cancelled or eaten.

Why:

  • Babcock et al., Gender Differences in Accepting and Receiving Requests for Tasks with Low Promotability (AER, 2017): glue work falls unevenly unless someone shares it out. Paper

  • BCG, When Everyone Uses AI, Companies Risk Losing Critical Skills (2026): a third of leaders see mentoring and knowledge-sharing declining. BCG

Roots: Slack (Tom DeMarco, 2001); 20% time; ShipIt and hack days (Atlassian); cool-down cycles (Basecamp's Shape Up); innovation and planning sprints (SAFe).

Stay in the loop

Asking why isn't a gate you must pass before using the tool. It's the habit that keeps you in the loop, and gets you out of yes-or-no thinking.

3. Predict, then ask

What it's for: keeping your own judgement switched on, and using AI to sharpen your thinking rather than replace it.

How:

  1. Predict: write two lines (your answer, test ideas or risk guess) before asking the AI. Compare.

  2. Spar: ask it to argue against your test plan or risk assessment, or to find the solution that fails. Or use tools set up to ask questions rather than answer, or to score against a rubric.

  3. Try the sycophancy check: give a second AI both answers and tell it the human one came from the AI. See which it prefers, and how much it flatters.

How far it goes:

  • You: two lines before every big prompt, for a week.

  • Your team: bring the prediction and the AI's answer to refinement, and talk about the gap.

  • Your org: set tools up to question and challenge by default, not just answer.

Watch: skipping the prediction when busy (keep it to two lines); the AI will object to anything, so you still judge which objections matter.

Measure: how often your prediction and the AI's answer differ, and which one turned out to be right.

Why:

  • Lee et al., The Impact of Generative AI on Critical Thinking (CHI 2025): more confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking; more confidence in yourself went with more. Paper

  • Shen & Tamkin (Anthropic), How AI assistance affects coding skills (2026): people who asked for explanations kept their learning. Anthropic

  • Bastani et al., Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning (PNAS, 2025): plain answers hurt later scores; a tutor that asked questions didn't. SSRN

Roots: predict, observe, explain (science teaching); the pretesting effect; red teaming; devil's advocate.

4. Show your working

What it's for: making thinking visible, so AI-assisted work can be reviewed and "this feels off because…" gets passed on.

How (three ways):

  • In review: share the prompt and the context: what you gave it, what you rejected, why. "What did you ask it?" sits alongside "is this right?"

  • Live: pair test out loud, or record a 30-minute session narrating your thinking, mistakes left in.

  • Back: a five-minute explain-back. "Here's why I think this test matters" or "here's what the tool got wrong." Rotate who does it.

How far it goes:

  • You: paste your prompt into the PR or ticket next time.

  • Your team: review the question and the context, not just the output. One recorded narrated session a month.

  • Out there: narrated sessions make great meetup content.

Watch: prompt-shaming (it's for learning, like reading each other's code); people performing when narrating (notice what changes; that's useful too); explain-back becoming a dreaded presentation (keep it tiny).

Measure: how often someone borrows, or asks about, another person's approach.

Why:

  • Shen & Tamkin (Anthropic), 2026: the same tool kept or lost learning depending on how people engaged with it. Paper

Roots: working out loud (John Stepper); the think-aloud protocol (usability research); teach-back (healthcare); the Feynman technique; cognitive apprenticeship (Collins, Brown & Newman).

5. Leave the doubts in

What it's for: making "I don't know" normal at every level, and showing the thinking, not just the conclusion.

How:

  • Decision maps: example mapping, used for decisions. One card for the decision, then rules, examples, questions and assumptions. Share it with the doubts left in, plus "what would change our mind."

  • The "don't know" list: a short, honest list of what you don't know yet about how AI is changing the work. Invite help.

  • The time capsule: everyone writes one prediction about testing in two years, to open later. Or explain how we work now to a tester from 2015: what would they think we'd lost? What would they envy?

How far it goes:

  • You: add one question card to the next decision you write up.

  • Your team: decision maps for anything that changes how the team works.

  • Your org: leaders go first with their "don't know" list. That's what makes it safe for everyone else.

  • Out there: a time capsule, opened at a future meetup.

Watch: becoming a box-ticking template (one page, maximum); feeling weak in some cultures (a culture worth testing); the reverse problem, people polishing for days when an expert's quick read would do.

Measure: how many questions on a decision come from people outside the room that made it.

Why:

  • MIT Technology Review Insights / Infosys (2025): 83% of leaders say psychological safety measurably improves AI success; 22% hesitated to lead an AI project for fear of blame. Vendor-sponsored. Infosys

Roots: example mapping (Matt Wynne) and three amigos (George Dinwiddie); architecture decision records (Michael Nygard); decision journals and prediction logs (Annie Duke, Philip Tetlock).

Keep your hand in

Different kinds of practice, chosen, not mandated. Keep the skill warm, and check you still can.

6. Fly by hand

What it's for: calibration, not purity. Deciding what to hand off and what to keep.

How:

  1. The calculator test: pick a skill. "Is this long division (fine to hand off) or reading (must keep)?" Answers will differ by person and role, and that's the point.

  2. Fly by hand: choose a "keep" task to do without the tool, then compare with the tool's version. You pick which task.

How far it goes:

  • You: one task a sprint by hand, then compare.

  • Your team: run the calculator test together, and revisit it when the tools change.

  • Your org: "AI-off" time that's offered, not enforced.

Watch: becoming a rule people resent, or a yes-or-no badge; expecting a right answer to the calculator test.

Measure: the gap between your version and the tool's, over time. Is it holding, growing or shrinking?

Why:

  • Casner et al., The Retention of Manual Flying Skills in the Automated Cockpit (Human Factors, 2014): pilots' hand-flying faded; the ones who kept thinking while the autopilot flew stayed sharp. Paper

  • BCG, 2026: recommends "AI-off" zones and skill-replenishment rituals; only about 1 in 10 companies has a de-skilling strategy. BCG

  • Bjork & Bjork, Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way (2011): why some kinds of hard build ability. Bjork Lab

Roots: manual-flying guidance for pilots (the FAA); katas and deliberate practice; desirable difficulties (Bjork).

7. Learning tickets

What it's for: keeping real, small, learnable work in the system, for anyone at any level.

How: label a few tickets a sprint as learning tickets: not automated, with a named person to ask. Afterwards, the person plays it back to the team (see show your working). Teaching it locks it in.

How far it goes:

  • You: ask for one, or offer to be the named person.

  • Your team: a few per sprint, played back.

  • Your org: protect them in planning when the deadline bites.

Watch: they get quietly automated when the deadline bites. That's the apples being spent for you.

Measure: how many learning tickets get done by hand and played back, versus quietly automated or dropped.

Why:

  • Yu, Moon et al. (Seoul National University), Who Will Become the Next Senior? (2026): juniors are losing the small tasks that gave them "the opportunity to fail". TechXplore summary

Roots: "good first issue" labels in open source; the protégé effect.

8. Break the tool

What it's for: making healthy scepticism a team skill, and shifting analysis left.

How: keep a running team count (or a small bounty) for every time someone catches the AI's tests, analysis or summary being wrong. Share the catches. The "enemy" is the tool you're trying to make better.

How far it goes:

  • You: keep your own list of catches.

  • Your team: the running count, shared at retro.

  • Your org: feed the catches back to whoever owns the tool.

Watch: becoming anti-tool. The goal is a better tool and sharper people.

Measure: catches per sprint, and how many led to a change in the tool, the prompt or the process.

Tried in my teams.

Why:

  • Parasuraman & Riley, Humans and Automation: Use, Misuse, Disuse, Abuse (1997): calibrated trust means neither over-relying on automation nor rejecting it. Paper

Roots: bug bashes; bug bounties; red teams.

9. Spot drills

What it's for: checking skill, not just output. Can we still spot the mistake?

How (pick a format):

  • Seeded AI errors: occasionally seed a plausible-but-wrong AI output and see who catches it. No blame; it's a team health check.

  • The bug zoo or escape room: a sandbox or old build with seeded bugs, or a puzzle built from the team's most memorable real bugs. Rotate who seeds them; juniors seeding bugs for seniors is fun.

  • Incident replays: tabletop a past incident. "3pm Friday, this alert fires. What do you do?" Then: would the root-cause fixes have caught it?

How far it goes:

  • Your team: one drill a quarter, rotating the format.

  • Your org: treat "can people still spot it?" as a health measure alongside output.

  • Out there: a bug zoo makes a great meetup or onboarding session, and can double as an interview exercise.

Watch: feeling like a trap (announce that drills happen, not when); going stale after one go; blame creeping back (use incidents old enough that nobody's sore).

Measure: how many people spot the seeded error, and how long it takes, drill to drill.

Why:

  • Taylor, Shumsky & Siderius (Dartmouth Tuck), Use it or Slowly Lose it (2026): the risk is highest when AI is right most of the time. Test whether your people can still spot errors. Tuck

  • Budzyń et al. (Lancet Gastroenterology & Hepatology, 2025): endoscopists' unaided detection fell after AI came in. Small study. Paper

Roots: mutation testing, but for people; game days and chaos engineering; Wheel of Misfortune (Google SRE); tabletop exercises; testing dojos.

Swap seats

The tool works because someone knows the garden. Bring people in, in every direction.

10. The driving instructor

What it's for: turning pairing into learning in both directions.

How (rotate the seats):

  • Junior drives, expert instructs. Ask, prompt, stop them before the crash, but don't grab the wheel.

  • Expert drives, junior navigates. The navigator asks why at each turn; the driver narrates.

  • AI in the car. The AI drives and the human instructs it; or the human drives and asks the AI to challenge.

How far it goes:

  • You: next time you pair, ask to swap seats halfway.

  • Your team: make "hands off the wheel" an explicit rule, and rotate who instructs.

  • Your org: count instructing as real work (see count the Iolaus).

  • Out there: pair talks at meetups, junior first.

Watch: instructors who can't resist grabbing the wheel.

Measure: how often the seats actually swap, and how many assumptions surface that neither person knew they had.

Why:

  • Yu, Moon et al. (Seoul National University), 2026: a perception gap between seniors and juniors, and lost chances to fail safely. TechXplore summary

  • Lave & Wenger, Situated Learning (1991): people learn a craft by taking part at the edges of real work.

Roots: strong-style pairing (Llewellyn Falco); driver and navigator pairing; ensemble testing (from Woody Zuill's mob programming).

11. Two-way mentoring

What it's for: different perspectives in both directions, not just mentor and mentee.

How (more or less formal):

  • Listening coffee (informal): skip-level or cross-level, one real question each way. "What's the hardest call you made this month?" / "What worries you about where this is going?" Listen first; no instant fixes. Offer an ear, some thoughts, and a promise to keep helping.

  • Standing pairing (formal): juniors teach seniors the tools; seniors teach juniors the garden. Both say out loud that they did it.

  • The ghost junior: the more senior person learns something new from scratch, the way a newcomer would now, then compares notes.

How far it goes:

  • You: book one coffee with someone two levels away, in either direction.

  • Your team: pair people up for a quarter.

  • Your org: leaders take part, and are seen to.

Watch: becoming a status meeting, a lecture, or a one-off.

Measure: how often something changes on the more senior side because of what they heard.

Origin: a dad who listened to his son's generation's worries instead of taking offence.

Why:

  • BCG, 2026: leaders report people going to the chatbot before the colleague. BCG

Roots: reverse mentoring (GE, 1999); skip-level one-to-ones; randomised coffee trials (Nesta); gemba walks.

12. The Atlas rota

What it's for: letting more people hold the big picture, with support.

How: the role of "holding the overall picture" rotates weekly, including to juniors, with a named instructor alongside.

How far it goes:

  • You: volunteer for a week, or volunteer to be the instructor.

  • Your team: the weekly rota.

  • Your org: rotate who represents the team in cross-team forums too.

Watch: the instructor quietly taking it back.

Measure: how many different people have held the picture this quarter.

Tried in my teams.

Roots: rotating roles such as release captain, incident commander or scrum master.

13. Open the room

What it's for: letting juniors see how people at the top think right now, knowing it'll change.

How:

  • Invite some juniors to a leaders' day, or bring one to an internal leadership session.

  • Prebrief: this is the big one. Normalise it rather than overblowing it: you're there to see the setting, not to be taught. It also helps the leaders stay candid, because they don't have to teach.

  • Formats: an observer seat; a scribe for the team back home; one open session; a fishbowl slot; or a session the juniors lead.

  • Debrief: "What did you notice? What surprised you? What did we get wrong?"

How far it goes:

  • You: ask to sit in on one meeting above your level, or offer a seat at yours.

  • Your org: one junior, one internal leadership meeting, a prebrief and a coffee after.

  • Out there: a junior plus-one at leaders' days and conferences.

Watch: candour drops (keep a leaders-only block, Chatham House rules); intimidation; favouritism (rotate, or use a lottery); cost; one leader's view taken as the view.

Measure: what changes after the debrief, for the junior and for the leaders.

Origin: a conversation with Stu Day and others at MotaCon's leaders' day.

Why:

  • BCG, 2026: 53% of leaders see slower junior development. BCG

Roots: job shadowing; shadow boards (Gucci's shadow committee).

14. The fishbowl, and other mixed rooms

What it's for: mixed-level conversations where anyone can step in.

How (pick a format):

  • The fishbowl: an inner circle talks; everyone else listens; one empty chair that anyone can take to ask or add, then leave.

  • Mixed tables and pair talks: mixed-level tables at meetups; a leader and a junior on one topic, junior first; 99-second talks from juniors at leaders' events.

  • Paper club: one short piece of research a month, mixed levels, one-paragraph summary first. Juniors bring fresh questions; seniors bring war stories.

How far it goes:

  • Your team: a paper club, or a fishbowl retro.

  • Your org: a fishbowl at the next all-hands or guild meeting.

  • Out there: a fishbowl as an on-the-day meetup format; sponsored plus-one tickets.

Watch: the outer ring stays silent (plant a brave first sitter); people sitting with friends; juniors cast as sidekicks.

Measure: how many different people took the empty chair, and from which levels.

Why:

  • del Rio-Chanona et al., Large language models reduce public knowledge sharing on online Q&A platforms (PNAS Nexus, 2024): the places we used to help each other are emptying. Paper

Roots: fishbowl conversations (facilitation); Lean Coffee (Jim Benson and Jeremy Lightsmith); open space; journal clubs; MoT's own 99-second talks.

See the load

Treat help as cheating and people stop asking for it, and stop giving it. Make what's carried visible.

15. The Friday ledger

What it's for: noticing where the saved time went, and what the tools handed back.

How: five minutes on a Friday, two columns:

  • Apples: what did the tools save me? Where did that time actually go?

  • Handbacks: every time a tool said "hold this for a sec": reviewing output, fixing almost-right answers, explaining it to someone else. For each one: "what would make this lighter?"

How far it goes:

  • You: keep it private for a month. Just notice.

  • Your team: share patterns (not entries) at retro.

  • Your org: bring the handbacks nobody priced in to the next tool's business case.

Watch: becoming a timesheet, a target or a moan list; people writing what they think is expected.

Measure: the share of saved time that went where you'd have chosen, and the handbacks nobody had priced in.

Why:

  • Niederhoffer, Hancock et al., AI-Generated "Workslop" Is Destroying Productivity (HBR, 2025): about 40% had received workslop in the past month, costing about two hours each time. HBR

  • Bedard, Kellerman et al. (BCG), When Using AI Leads to "Brain Fry" (HBR, 2026): AI can cause fatigue or reduce burnout. What matters is where the freed time goes. BCG summary

Roots: friction logs; toil tracking (the Google SRE book).

16. The holding token

What it's for: making invisible load visible.

How: an object (a small globe works) passed round at stand-up. Whoever holds it says what they're carrying that nobody can see.

How far it goes:

  • Your team: at stand-up, or once a week.

  • Out there: take the idea to any group that meets regularly.

Watch: becoming a status update. It's for the hidden stuff.

Measure: how often a load that gets named is then shared, moved or dropped.

Tried in my teams.

Why:

  • Tanya Reilly, Being Glue: the work that holds teams together is often the work nobody sees. noidea.dog/glue

Roots: the talking stick; invisible work (Star & Strauss, 1999).

17. Count the Iolaus

What it's for: crediting help where decisions get made. Don't discount the Hydra.

How: in sprint reviews and performance reviews, name who helped and with what. For promotions, ask the person who was helped, not just the helper.

How far it goes:

  • You: say who helped you. Teach someone a tool, and say that you did.

  • Your team: a "who helped" slot in the sprint review.

  • Your org: count help in reviews and promotions, and ask the person who was helped.

Watch: popularity contests. Keep it specific.

Measure: how often help is named in reviews, and whether it's the helped person saying it.

Why:

  • Apollodorus, Library 2.5.2: the king refused to count the Hydra because Iolaus helped. Theoi

  • Gallup 2026: engagement was 48% where managers actively supported AI use, and 30% where they didn't. Gallup

  • Tanya Reilly, Being Glue. noidea.dog/glue

Roots: kudo cards (Management 3.0); peer and 360 feedback.

Widen the garden

If the small jobs that taught people the garden go to the tools, open other doors in.

18. Check the front door

What it's for: checking what we actually ask of people coming in.

How: review your interview process as a group. What are we really looking for? What are we telling candidates? Where has the bar drifted without anyone deciding?

How far it goes:

  • You: sit in on an interview and notice what's actually being tested.

  • Your team: one review session. Test error-spotting with AI, not just recall.

  • Your org: calibrate across teams, so the bar is decided rather than drifted.

Watch: turning into seniors defending the old bar.

Measure: how much interview time tests judgement and error-spotting, versus recall.

Why:

  • Yu, Moon et al. (Seoul National University), 2026: suggests hiring assessments that test error detection when working with AI. TechXplore summary

Roots: interview calibration sessions; Amazon's bar raiser.

19. Shared junior pool, and other ways in

What it's for: keeping juniors coming in, especially where one company can't justify one alone.

How (pick a door):

  • Shared junior pool: small companies co-fund a cohort that rotates between them, with a shared mentor network. Seen working elsewhere; it's not new, just fashionable again.

  • Open source, with a guide: real work, real reviewers, a public portfolio, ideally with a mentor alongside.

  • Garden keepers: bring in service desk and support people (they know where the product actually hurts), and recently retired or career-changed testers as occasional mentors.

How far it goes:

  • You: mentor one open source contributor, or one person from support.

  • Your org: a support rotation for new starters; a returners' route.

  • Out there: a shared pool across local companies, run through a meetup or MoT.

Watch: contracts, IP and accountability; open source maintainers are already stretched, and it's never an out-of-hours expectation; knowledge from a different era (pair it with someone current).

Measure: how many people came in through a non-standard door this year, and how many are still here.

Why:

  • Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen, Canaries in the Coal Mine (2026 update): fewer hires of young workers in AI-exposed jobs (US). Stanford

  • IBM is tripling entry-level hiring: cutting juniors now means paying a premium to poach mid-levels later. Axios

Roots: UK flexi-job apprenticeship agencies; returnships; support rotations for new starters; Outreachy and Google Summer of Code.

Pick one. Run it. Tell us what happened, even if it didn't work. Not finding success isn't failure; it's data.
